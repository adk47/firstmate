## The short version (read this first)

- **We delete** – account data on request: email, login credentials,
  subscription and billing records (subject to statutory retention), and
  usage/behavioral data.
- **We generally keep** – factual music credits: performer, writer, producer,
  and engineering credits on published works. GDPR's erasure right has
  exceptions, and we rely on them. See the legal basis below.
- **Always, no exceptions** – respond within **1 month** of receipt. Ignoring
  a request is itself a violation, even when we'd be entitled to refuse.
- **Always, no exceptions** – log every request in the DSAR log, and complete
  a balancing memo for every refusal. The memo is our accountability evidence.

**Before anything else:** verify the requester's identity. Never confirm,
discuss, or delete data for an unverified requester.

## Why we can keep credits (legal basis)

**Art. 17(3)(a) – Freedom of expression and information.** The right to
erasure does not apply where processing is necessary for exercising the right
of freedom of expression and information. Muso is a factual, historical
archive of attribution on published works – comparable to film credit
databases and bibliographic records.

**Art. 21(1) – Compelling legitimate grounds.** Where a requester objects to
legitimate-interest processing, we may retain the data if compelling
legitimate grounds override their interests. Ours: the data is *professional*
rather than private-life, was made public through the release of the works
themselves, is factual and verifiable, and each credit is part of an
interconnected record involving other collaborators whose interests deletion
would prejudice.

**What our position is *not*:** "it's public information." Publicly available
personal data is still personal data under GDPR. The public nature of the
works is one factor in the balancing test – never the whole answer, and never
the reply we send.

## Request handling flow (step by step)

1. **Intake.** Log the request in the DSAR log with the date received. The
   one-month clock starts now.
2. **Verify identity.** Confirm the requester controls the email or profile in
   question. No verification means no action and no discussion of what data we
   hold.
3. **Split the request.** Account data (email, login, billing, usage) is
   deleted, subject to statutory billing retention. Credit records go to the
   balancing test.
4. **Check escalation triggers.** See the list below. Any trigger goes to
   counsel, not the template.
5. **Complete the balancing memo.** Required for every refusal. Template
   below – takes ten minutes.
6. **Send the response letter.** Within one month of receipt. Template below.
7. **Offer the softer measures.** Rectification, profile unlink/deactivation,
   search de-indexing – proportionate offers strengthen the refusal.
8. **Log the outcome.** Letter, memo, and dates filed in the DSAR log.

**Escalate to counsel – do not use the template – if the requester:**

- claims the credits are inaccurate or fraudulent
- is or may be a minor
- raises safety concerns (stalking, harassment, protected identity)
- invokes special category data
- is represented by counsel or references a supervisory authority

## Team FAQ (common questions)

**"The data is public anyway – why can't we just say that?"**
Because publicly available personal data is still personal data under GDPR.
Our refusal rests on the Art. 17(3)(a) exception and the balancing test – the
public nature of the works is one factor in that test, not the whole answer,
and never the reply we send.

**They want the profile down but don't mind the credits staying.**
The easy case: deactivate or unlink the profile, delete account data, keep
credits. No refusal letter needed – just confirm the actions taken, within the
one-month window.

**The credit is actually wrong.**
That's rectification (Art. 16), not erasure – fix it. There is essentially no
exception to correcting inaccurate data, and it aligns with the product's
value anyway. If they frame the inaccuracy as fraud, escalate to counsel.

**The requester is in the US, not the EU.**
This playbook covers GDPR and UK GDPR only. US state laws (CCPA/CPRA and
others) have their own deletion rights with different exemptions – flag those
requests to the playbook owner separately.

**We're going to miss the one-month deadline.**
Complex requests can be extended by up to two further months – but only if we
notify the requester of the extension, with reasons, *within the first month*.
Silence is a violation regardless of the merits.
